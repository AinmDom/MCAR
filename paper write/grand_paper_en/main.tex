\title{Sparse HRTF Interpolation via MCA Residual Learning and Conditional Implicit Representation}
\author{Young}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{csmtAbstract}
Sparse head-related transfer function (HRTF) interpolation often fails to recover fine magnitude details. This paper proposes FiLM-conditioned SIREN with Spectral CNN (FSC). Starting from magnitude-corrected and time-aligned interpolation (MCA), FSC uses a subject-level conditional embedding to modulate a continuous direction--frequency residual field and employs a binaural spectral CNN to refine local spectral and interaural structure. The reconstructed magnitude is finally combined with the MCA phase to recover head-related impulse responses (HRIRs). On 44 SONICOM test subjects under the Q26 protocol, FSC achieved full-sphere ERB, contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB, ERB-band ILD, ITD-weighted MAE, and full-sphere LSD values of 0.8360, 1.2280, 1.6514\,dB, 18.8480\,$\mu$s, and 3.6029\,dB, respectively. Relative to MCA, the mean errors decreased on all four magnitude or spectral metrics, whereas ITD-weighted MAE increased slightly. Across three independent seeds, the validation-set CNN ablation improved all eight metrics and placed all 24 paired-bootstrap intervals below zero. In the sparsity experiment, every reported mean error decreased as the observation density increased from Q14 through Q26 to Q50. After training from scratch on the ARI database, FSC improved all five primary metrics over MCA for all 22 test subjects. These results show that FSC can compensate for residual MCA magnitude errors and that the effect can be reproduced after retraining on a second database; zero-shot cross-database generalization and perceptual benefits remain to be established.
\end{csmtAbstract}

\begin{keywords}
head-related transfer function; HRTF interpolation; MCA; residual learning; conditional implicit representation; interaural cues
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A head-related transfer function (HRTF) describes the direction-dependent acoustic transfer from a sound source to the two eardrums and underpins binaural spatial-audio rendering. Its time-domain counterpart, the head-related impulse response (HRIR), contains the interaural time difference (ITD), interaural level difference (ILD), and monaural spectral cues produced by the pinnae, head, and torso\cite{R6}. Because human morphology varies substantially, directly using nonindividualized HRTFs may introduce spatial-cue mismatch. Classic localization experiments showed that such mismatch can increase front--back and up--down confusions and reduce elevation accuracy for some listeners\cite{R27}. Dense individualized HRTFs normally require direction-by-direction measurements in a controlled acoustic environment, making acquisition costly. Interpolating a dense HRTF set from a small number of measured directions is therefore an important means of reducing the measurement burden of personalization\cite{R13}.

Sparse HRTF interpolation estimates binaural responses at unmeasured directions from a limited set of observations. Existing approaches can broadly be divided into neighboring-direction weighting, functional models, and learning-based methods\cite{R15}. The first two categories include linear or bilinear interpolation, vector-base amplitude panning (VBAP), inverse-distance weighting (IDW), principal-component analysis, filter models, and spherical-harmonic (SH) expansion\cite{R2,R3}. Learning-based methods infer HRTFs at target directions from sparse observations using structures such as autoencoders and neural fields\cite{R4,R5}. Traditional methods offer clear physical or geometric interpretations, whereas learning-based methods provide stronger nonlinear representation capacity. Both nevertheless face a common difficulty: contralateral high-frequency responses change rapidly with direction, and local spectral peaks and notches together with binaural cues are often harder to recover than the overall low-frequency magnitude.

Time alignment removes direction-dependent propagation delays before interpolation and thereby reduces the difficulty caused by rapidly varying phase\cite{R3}. Magnitude-corrected and time-aligned interpolation (MCA) further introduces a magnitude-correction filter derived from a frequency-smoothed representation, so that delay compensation, coarse spectral reconstruction, and phase recovery are already handled by an explicit signal-processing pipeline\cite{R1}. Nevertheless, MCA can still leave structured direction-dependent magnitude errors, especially at contralateral high frequencies and around local spectral peaks and notches.

This observation motivates a residual rather than a direct-reconstruction formulation. Instead of asking a neural model to relearn the complete HRTF, including components already represented by MCA, we retain MCA as an explicit reconstruction prior and learn only the remaining magnitude error. The target HRTF magnitude is therefore decomposed into an MCA baseline and a learned residual. This separation preserves the existing phase and reconstruction chain while concentrating the learned component on spectral structures that remain insufficiently reconstructed by the conventional interpolator.

The remaining MCA error exhibits two complementary forms of structure. First, it varies continuously with source direction and frequency and is also subject dependent, which motivates a shared continuous residual representation modulated by subject-specific information. Second, fine HRTF structure is strongly local in frequency: neighboring bins jointly form spectral peaks and notches, while the two ears exhibit coupled spectral relationships. A purely coordinate-wise implicit representation does not explicitly encode these local cross-frequency interactions.

Based on these observations, we propose FiLM-conditioned SIREN with Spectral CNN (FSC). A conditional encoder summarizes subject-specific acoustic characteristics, and feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM)\cite{R7} modulates a sinusoidal representation network (SIREN)\cite{R11} that represents the continuous MCA residual field over direction and frequency. A binaural spectral CNN is then applied to the full left--right spectra to refine local cross-frequency and interaural structure. The two components therefore play complementary roles: the conditional implicit field models continuous subject-dependent residual variation, whereas the spectral CNN models local spectral neighborhoods that are not explicitly represented by independent coordinate queries. The model predicts only an MCA magnitude residual, and reconstruction reuses the MCA phase and the unpredicted frequency bins.

A second design consideration concerns the optimization target. Point-wise magnitude errors on a linear frequency grid treat FFT bins uniformly, whereas auditory frequency resolution is nonuniform and is more naturally organized in perceptual frequency bands. We therefore introduce an ERB-oriented proxy term during optimization so that residual refinement is also constrained at an auditory-band scale. Final evaluation uses a strict HRIR-domain ERB measure rather than the training proxy itself. ERB is used here as an auditory-band error representation, not as a substitute for perceptual listening evaluation; ILD, ITD, and LSD are therefore retained as complementary metrics.

The main contributions are as follows. (1) We formulate sparse HRTF interpolation as residual refinement over an explicit MCA reconstruction rather than direct prediction of the complete HRTF. The conventional time-alignment, magnitude-correction, phase, and reconstruction chain is retained, while the learned component is devoted to the structured magnitude error that remains after MCA. (2) We propose a complementary continuous-and-local residual architecture. A subject-conditioned FiLM-SIREN represents the continuous MCA residual field over direction and frequency, while a binaural spectral CNN explicitly refines local cross-frequency and interaural spectral structure. This design separates continuous coordinate representation from local spectral-neighborhood modeling instead of assigning both roles to a single predictor. (3) We introduce an auditory-band-oriented residual-learning objective by incorporating a differentiable ERB proxy during training and using strict HRIR-domain ERB error as a central evaluation dimension, while retaining ILD, ITD, and LSD as complementary measures. (4) We validate these design choices under a unified protocol through comparisons with conventional and learning-based methods, paired CNN ablation, independently trained Q14/Q26/Q50 sparsity experiments, and independent retraining on the ARI database.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Conventional HRTF Interpolation}

Spherical-harmonic expansion and neighboring-direction weighting are common conventional approaches to sparse HRTF interpolation. Time alignment can reduce the spatial order required by an SH representation\cite{R3}. SUpDEq improves spatial reconstruction through directional equalization before interpolation and inverse equalization afterwards, and its performance under different spherical sampling schemes has also been studied\cite{R2,R2S}. MCA constructs a magnitude-correction filter using the interpolated smoothed magnitude as a reference on top of time-aligned interpolation\cite{R1}. Rather than treating these conventional methods as baselines that must be replaced completely, this study uses MCA as an interpretable initial reconstruction and specifically learns its remaining magnitude error.

\subsection{Learning-Based HRTF Interpolation}

HRTF personalization may rely on anthropometric information or sparse acoustic measurements; the two tasks provide different forms of listener-specific information\cite{R15,R16}. We focus on the latter setting, in which a target listener already has a small set of measured HRTFs. HRTF Field maps source direction to continuous HRTF magnitude with a neural field, providing a unified representation across spatial sampling schemes\cite{R22}. The spherical neural-process meta-learner of Thuillier et al. conditions on sparse HRTF observations from the same subject and queries unobserved directions on the unit sphere while also estimating predictive uncertainty\cite{R26}. FSP-AE interprets regularized linear regression as an autoencoder and jointly processes source direction, frequency, and input type to model magnitude and ITD\cite{R4}. RANF combines retrieved HRTFs, convolution, and bidirectional sequence modeling and can be parameter-efficiently adapted to a target subject\cite{R5}. These approaches show that nonlinear models and continuous representations can effectively support HRTF interpolation. The distinction in this work is therefore not the use of a neural interpolator itself, but the function being learned: FSC places an explicit conventional reconstruction underneath the learned model and represents the remaining magnitude error relative to that reconstruction. MCA thus defines the signal-processing reference surface, while the learned model performs structured residual refinement and retains the MCA phase and reconstruction path. Because the methods differ in subject adaptation, phase recovery, and output representation, we evaluate their final reconstructions under a common pipeline rather than mixing scores reported in the original papers with our reevaluated results.

Cross-database learning additionally faces differences between measurement systems. Even after harmonizing sampling rate, HRIR length, common directions, and magnitude scale, Pauwels and Picinali found that machine-learning models could identify the source HRTF database with high accuracy, indicating that measurement-chain characteristics may create database-specific systematic differences\cite{R24}. Wen et al. further analyzed direction-dependent system-response differences caused by loudspeakers, microphones, and measurement environments, and proposed frequency-response normalization to reduce database identifiability and improve unified cross-database representation learning\cite{R25}. These findings also motivate a distinction between independent retraining and replication on another database and zero-shot cross-database generalization.

\subsection{Conditional Implicit Representations and Spectral Modeling}

FiLM modulates intermediate features using conditionally generated feature-wise scales and shifts\cite{R7}, whereas SIREN uses periodic activations to represent continuous signals and their high-frequency detail\cite{R11}. These properties make their combination suitable for a subject-dependent residual function whose independent variables include continuous source direction and frequency. However, coordinate-based implicit prediction does not by itself explicitly enforce local relationships among neighboring frequency bins. Such relationships are important for HRTF spectral peaks and notches, and binaural reconstruction additionally involves coupled left--right spectral structure. Convolutional networks provide an explicit local-neighborhood inductive bias through local connectivity and shared kernels\cite{R21}. FSC therefore assigns continuous residual representation to FiLM-SIREN and local binaural spectral refinement to the subsequent spectral CNN, rather than using the CNN as an unexplained additional module.

The spectral objective is also motivated by auditory frequency resolution. Equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) provides an auditory-filter-based frequency scale whose bandwidth increases with frequency\cite{R23}. This motivates describing spectral deviation not only at individual FFT bins but also after integration over auditory-frequency bands. In FSC, ERB is therefore used as an optimization and evaluation scale: a differentiable proxy is used during training, whereas the reported strict ERB error is computed from the reconstructed HRIR. This distinction avoids treating the training surrogate itself as the final evaluation criterion. Because objective errors and subjective spatial-audio perception are not in one-to-one correspondence\cite{R18}, spectral, ILD, and ITD results are reported separately, and a reduction in any single metric is not interpreted directly as improved localization or externalization.

\section{MCA Residual Learning with a Conditional Implicit Representation}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overall Framework and Learning Target}

This study begins with magnitude-corrected and time-aligned interpolation (MCA) and learns the HRTF magnitude residual that remains in its interpolated result. MCA augments time-aligned interpolation with magnitude correction based on a frequency-smoothed representation\cite{R1}. We retain this conventional reconstruction as the baseline and use subject information from sparse observations to predict an additive residual instead of directly regressing a complete complex HRTF. This decomposition is not merely an initialization strategy: it fixes MCA as an explicit signal-processing prior throughout both training and inference and defines the learned function as the correction required relative to that prior. FSC modulates a continuous MCA residual field with a subject-level conditional embedding and integrates three modules: FiLM-based conditional modulation, a SIREN implicit residual representation, and binaural spectral refinement with a CNN. Along a single forward path, the modules generate subject conditions, represent direction--frequency residuals, and perform cross-frequency binaural correction. Stage-wise optimization is only a training strategy and does not change the definition of the complete model as the combination of all three modules.

Let $s$ denote a subject, $\mathbf q=(x,y,z)$ a unit query-direction vector, $e\in\{L,R\}$ an ear, and $f$ a frequency. The $Q$ observed directions of subject $s$ form the set $\mathcal O_s$. Let $M^\star_{s,e}(\mathbf q,f)$ and $M^{\mathrm{MCA}}_{s,e}(\mathbf q,f)$ be the reference and MCA magnitudes in dB. The supervision target is
\begin{equation}
r^\star_{s,e}=M^\star_{s,e}-M^{\mathrm{MCA}}_{s,e},\qquad
\widetilde r^\star_{s,e}=\frac{r^\star_{s,e}-\mu_r}{\sigma_r}.
\label{eq:residual-target}
\end{equation}
The statistics $\mu_r$ and $\sigma_r$ are determined only from the training set. Reference magnitudes at observed directions are used as conditional inputs only within $\mathcal O_s$; reference magnitudes at unobserved directions serve as training supervision or for previously specified evaluation and are never inference inputs. MCA magnitudes and the original MCA correction features are likewise constructed only from the corresponding sparse observations.

Figure~\ref{fig:FSC-reconstruction-workflow} summarizes the complete inference chain. MCA provides the baseline magnitude, phase, and magnitude-correction features, while FSC predicts magnitude residuals at the 463 modeled frequency bins. The remaining 50 single-sided bins retain their MCA values, and the corrected magnitude is combined with MCA phase before conjugate extension, a 1024-point IFFT, and cropping to 256 HRIR samples. Reference HRTFs at unobserved directions are not used as inference inputs.

The following subsections describe the FiLM, SIREN, and CNN modules in the order in which they appear along this unified forward path.

\subsection{Subject Conditioning and Continuous Residual Representation}

The FiLM module first compresses a subject's sparse binaural spatial observations into a fixed-dimensional embedding and then uses it to modulate every sinusoidal layer of the SIREN. For each observed direction, the conditional encoder applies two one-dimensional convolutional layers to the normalized binaural spectrum, concatenates the direction vector $\mathbf q$, and projects the result to a 128-dimensional feature. Features are averaged over the valid-direction mask and linearly mapped to the subject-level condition $\mathbf z_s\in\mathbb R^{128}$. The binaural spatial characteristics of different subjects are thus encoded in a common 128-dimensional conditional space. During training, shared SIREN parameters are combined with $\mathbf z_s$ to represent individual differences, while the set encoder avoids using reference magnitudes at unobserved directions as input.

Following the conditional-control principle of feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM)\cite{R7}, a shared modulator generates frequency scaling, phase shift, and amplitude scaling parameters for SIREN layer $\ell$ from $\mathbf z_s$:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\gamma_\ell=1+0.5\tanh(\mathbf g_\ell),\quad
\boldsymbol\beta_\ell=\pi\tanh(\mathbf b'_\ell),\quad
\mathbf a_\ell=1+0.5\tanh(\mathbf a'_\ell).
\label{eq:film-modulation}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf g_\ell$, $\mathbf b'_\ell$, and $\mathbf a'_\ell$ are generated from $\mathbf z_s$ by a shared hidden layer and layer-specific output heads. Modulation is applied to every sinusoidal layer, so the same query coordinates can yield different residual representations under different subject conditions.

The SIREN module represents the MCA magnitude residual as a continuous function of direction and frequency. Its seven-dimensional query input is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf u_{s,\mathbf q,f}=
[x,y,z,\nu_{\mathrm{lin}}(f),\nu_{\mathrm{ERB}}(f),
 \widetilde M^{\mathrm{MCA}}_{s,L},\widetilde M^{\mathrm{MCA}}_{s,R}]^\top .
\end{equation}
The coordinates $\nu_{\mathrm{lin}}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{ERB}}$ map linear frequency and equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) frequency, respectively, to $[-1,1]$. The fundamental relationship between auditory-filter bandwidth and the ERB-rate scale is described by Moore and Glasberg\cite{R23}. The MCA magnitudes marked by tildes are normalized using separate training-set means and standard deviations. The global subject condition from FiLM and the local MCA magnitude at a query location therefore control residual prediction through distinct inputs.

This module adopts the periodic activation of a sinusoidal representation network (SIREN)\cite{R11}. It contains six sinusoidal layers with 256 hidden units each, modulated by the parameters generated by FiLM:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf h_\ell=\mathbf a_\ell(\mathbf z_s)\odot
\sin\!\left[
\omega_\ell\boldsymbol\gamma_\ell(\mathbf z_s)\odot
(\mathbf W_\ell\mathbf h_{\ell-1}+\mathbf b_\ell)
+\boldsymbol\beta_\ell(\mathbf z_s)\right].
\label{eq:film-siren}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf h_0=\mathbf u$, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $\omega_\ell=20$ for every layer. A final linear layer outputs the initial normalized residuals for the two ears, $\widetilde{\mathbf r}_F=F_\phi(\mathbf u,\mathbf z_s)$, which also form part of the full-spectrum CNN input. The continuous coordinate representation of SIREN models the residual across directions and frequencies, after which the CNN further captures local spectral and interaural structure.

\subsection{Binaural Spectral Refinement}

For each query direction, the CNN jointly processes the complete target-frequency band of both ears. The input matrix $X_s(\mathbf q)\in\mathbb R^{7\times F}$ contains, in order, the left-ear MCA magnitude, left-ear MCA magnitude-correction feature, left-ear FiLM-modulated SIREN residual, their right-ear counterparts, and normalized log-frequency. The MCA magnitude-correction feature is denoted by $\widetilde C^{\mathrm{MCA}}$ and is not the newly learned residual increment. The input order is
\begin{equation}
X_s(\mathbf q)=
[\widetilde M_L^{\mathrm{MCA}},
 \widetilde C_L^{\mathrm{MCA}},\widetilde r_{F,L},
 \widetilde M_R^{\mathrm{MCA}},
 \widetilde C_R^{\mathrm{MCA}},\widetilde r_{F,R},
 \nu_{\log}]^\top .
\label{eq:cnn-input}
\end{equation}
Each item is a frequency sequence of length $F$. The log-frequency coordinate $\nu_{\log}$ is specific to the CNN input and does not replace the joint linear/ERB frequency coordinates used by SIREN.

The binaural spectral convolutional neural network first maps the seven input channels to 48 channels and then applies four dilated depthwise-convolution residual blocks. The kernel length is 7 and the dilation rates are $1,2,4,8$. Within each block, pointwise convolutions expand the channels to 96 and project them back to 48, together with group normalization and SiLU activation. A two-layer, 64-dimensional mapping of the direction vector $\mathbf q$ supplies feature-wise scales and shifts to each block. A final $1\times1$ convolution outputs the spectral increment $\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf r}_\theta$ for both ears. The module explicitly exploits adjacent frequency bins and binaural spectral information, and combines with the FiLM and SIREN modules to obtain the final residual; its isolated effect must still be distinguished from those of the training objective and optimization budget.

\subsection{Training Objective and Reconstruction}

The FSC prediction and its conversion to dB are
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf r}_H=\widetilde{\mathbf r}_F+
C_\theta(X_s(\mathbf q),\mathbf q),\qquad
\mathbf r_H^{\mathrm{dB}}=\sigma_r\widetilde{\mathbf r}_H+\mu_r.
\end{equation}
The CNN increment in dB is therefore $\sigma_r\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf r}_\theta$ and does not receive another addition of $\mu_r$. The reconstructed FSC magnitude is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf M}=\mathbf M^{\mathrm{MCA}}+
\mathbf r_H^{\mathrm{dB}}.
\end{equation}
To recover an HRIR, the complex spectrum at each selected frequency is constructed from $10^{\widehat M/20}$ and the corresponding MCA phase, while the remaining bins retain the original complex MCA values. After conjugate-symmetric extension and a 1024-point inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT), the first 256 HRIR samples are retained. The model does not learn phase, and every HRIR-based binaural-cue evaluation is performed after this complete reconstruction chain.

Inference always uses the FiLM, SIREN, and CNN modules together, whereas training is stage-wise. The first stage obtains the E130 parameters of the FiLM conditional-modulation and SIREN residual-representation modules. In the second stage, those modules and the conditional encoder are frozen and only the CNN is optimized. The weights and biases of the CNN output convolution are initialized to zero, so $\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf r}_\theta$ is identically zero at the start of stage two and the initial function of the complete FSC equals that of the stage-one model. Zero initialization applies only to the CNN output layer; it neither sets every CNN weight to zero nor makes the initial output equal to MCA.

The training objective combines a SmoothL1 loss on the normalized residual with seven auxiliary terms:
\begin{align}
\mathcal L={}&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{res}}+
\frac{1}{\sigma_r}\big(
0.75\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ERB\text{-}proxy}}+
0.25\mathcal L_{\mathrm{contraHF}}+
0.75\mathcal L_{\mathrm{strictILD}}\notag\\
&\qquad+
0.05\mathcal L_{\mathrm{bandILD}}+
0.25\mathcal L_{\Delta f}+
0.15\mathcal L_{\Delta^2 f}+
0.30\mathcal L_{\mathrm{notch}}\big).
\label{eq:training-loss}
\end{align}
The SmoothL1 threshold of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{res}}$ is 1 in normalized units. The remaining terms are divided by the training residual standard deviation $\sigma_r$ as implemented. Solid-angle weights are renormalized within the direction subset of each term and then averaged over ears and the relevant frequency bins or bands.

The ERB proxy is the central spectral term in the composite objective. Forty-one centers are uniformly spaced on the ERB axis corresponding to 50--20000\,Hz; triangular weights integrate the linear power of selected frequency bins, after which differences between band energies in dB are evaluated. The band definition follows the auditory-filter bandwidth and ERB-rate concepts\cite{R23}; the 41 centers and triangular weights form a differentiable approximation used here for backpropagation. This design aligns training more closely with the auditory-band error emphasized in this paper, although the final claims are based on strict HRIR-domain ERB evaluation. The term $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{contraHF}}$ is the magnitude absolute error above 10\,kHz in the contralateral hemisphere. These two terms constrain band energy and absolute high-frequency magnitude, respectively, but the former is not the strict HRIR-domain ERB metric reported in the final evaluation.

Local spectral structure is modeled by adjacent-bin differences at 4\,kHz and above. Let $\epsilon_f=\widehat M_f-M^\star_f$ be the magnitude-error sequence. The first- and second-order terms average $|\epsilon_{f+1}-\epsilon_f|$ and $|\epsilon_{f+2}-2\epsilon_{f+1}+\epsilon_f|$, respectively. Every bin in a difference stencil must lie within the selected band. Their units are reported as dB/bin and dB/bin$^2$, rather than as derivatives with respect to continuous frequency. The multiscale spectral-notch term compares
\begin{equation}
D_r(M)_f=\tau\log\!\left[
1+\exp\!\left(
\frac{(M_{f-r}+M_{f+r})/2-M_f-\eta}{\tau}
\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where the radius is $r\in\{4,8,16\}$ frequency bins, the center frequency lies between 4 and 18\,kHz, $\eta=1$\,dB, and $\tau=0.5$\,dB. Absolute differences between depth maps are averaged across all radii.

The binaural terms are computed on independently sampled horizontal-plane directions. The strict interaural level difference (ILD) loss uses the left-to-right energy ratio of the reconstructed HRIR. The ERB-band ILD loss instead uses the left--right power difference in proxy ERB bands with center frequencies between 200 and 18000\,Hz and applies a SmoothL1 threshold of 0.5\,dB. Independent sampling ensures that horizontal-plane directions contribute to the binaural terms in every step; the constraints do not guarantee that every corresponding test error will decrease.

Each training epoch traverses 262 training subjects and performs one parameter update per subject. For each subject, 16 interpolation directions are sampled without replacement from the full measurement domain and another 16 from the horizontal plane. AdamW is used with a learning rate and weight decay of $10^{-4}$, a gradient-clipping threshold of 5, and FP32 training. The learning rate is linearly warmed up for two epochs and then cosine-decayed according to a 200-epoch schedule.

The stage-two training budget is fixed at 190 epochs (E190). During development, the validation-optimal epochs across three independent seeds were 190, 190, and 170. The prespecified median rule therefore selected 190 epochs as the common training budget. The endpoint parameters after epoch 190 are used, without resetting the budget or model parameters according to test performance.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}

The experiments use measured HRTFs from the SONICOM dataset\cite{R6}, stored in the Spatially Oriented Format for Acoustics (SOFA)\cite{R8}. The file variant is \texttt{FreeFieldCompMinPhase\_44kHz}, sampled at 44.1\,kHz; each subject contains binaural 256-sample HRIRs at 793 directions. Here, ``MinPhase'' is part of the free-field-compensated filename and does not mean that the complete HRTF is constrained to minimum phase in this study. The original SONICOM paper described an earlier 120-subject release. Based on a later official metadata snapshot and anomaly records, we retained 350 usable subjects.

Subjects are stratified by their free-field equalization files and divided into 262 training, 44 validation, and 44 test subjects, with no subject shared across splits. Normalization statistics are computed from the training set. Table~\ref{tab:protocol} summarizes the principal settings. Reference elevations range from $-45^\circ$ to $90^\circ$, so ``full sphere'' throughout this paper denotes the measured domain provided by the dataset and excludes the unmeasured lower spherical cap.

Q26 is a 26-direction subset constructed on the existing measurement grid and is not treated as an exact Lebedev grid. The selection fixes the north pole and one median-plane direction and then chooses 12 left--right symmetric pairs to maximize the minimum great-circle separation, with the log-determinant of a regularized third-order SH design used to resolve ties. Observed directions are excluded from evaluation, and every learning-based method obtains subject information only from its corresponding sparse input.

The architecture, training objective, and training budget are determined during the training and validation stages. Unless otherwise stated, experiments use the single E190 model selected in advance on the validation set; no model ensemble is used, and the test set is not involved in model selection.

To examine reproducibility on a second database, we additionally use the public semi-anechoic in-ear HRTF database of the Acoustics Research Institute (ARI), Austrian Academy of Sciences\cite{R20}. The experiment fixes the 177 \texttt{hrtf b} SOFA files available at download time. Of these, 174 have a consistent $1550\times2\times256$ shape, whereas three files with incomplete direction counts are excluded before any response values are read. The 174 eligible subjects are assigned by a fixed split to 130 training, 22 validation, and 22 test subjects. The original 48\,kHz HRIRs are converted to 44.1\,kHz using fixed polyphase resampling and right-padded to 256 samples; measurement delays are preserved and no additional time alignment is performed.

The ARI elevation range is $-30^\circ$ to $80^\circ$ and cannot contain every SONICOM-Q26 direction. Therefore, the external replication constructs an independent mirror-symmetric ARI-Q26 in advance using only public direction metadata, with all 26 observations taken from original measurement points. These observed points are excluded from evaluation, and solid-angle weights are recomputed over the remaining 1524 directions. ERB-band ILD uses the 86 unobserved horizontal-plane directions, while contralateral-$25^\circ$ regions and normalized weights are defined separately for the two ears. Because ARI and SONICOM use different measurement grids, Q26 sets, and directional weights, their absolute metrics are not pooled into a single experiment.

\subsection{Compared Methods}

The comparison covers seven external methods and FSC: SH only, SUpDEq SH, SUpDEq Natural Neighbor, SUpDEq Barycentric, MCA, FSP-AE, and RANF form the external method set. The analysis first tests residual compensation relative to MCA and then compares the learning-based methods.

Conventional reconstructions use the existing SUpDEq/MCA implementation. SH-related settings are third order, a head radius of 0.09\,m, and an SH regularization parameter of 0.01; natural-neighbor and barycentric interpolation retain their respective frozen definitions. All eight methods in the primary comparison use the same test subjects, Q26 observations, and interpolation-evaluation mask, without forcing them to share the same training or inference mechanism.

For FSC, which predicts only an MCA magnitude residual, evaluation uses the MCA phase and unpredicted-bin reuse chain described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Methods that directly generate HRIRs retain their native outputs, which are mapped to the common evaluation representation and frequency bins. All eight methods use the same test subjects, Q26 observation set, and evaluation mask, and the 12 reported metrics are computed from their complete reconstructed outputs.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The main text emphasizes five lower-is-better metrics. Full-sphere ERB error (FullSphereERB) is computed with the \texttt{AKerbError} function in AKtools\cite{R12}, over 50\,Hz to the 22.05\,kHz Nyquist frequency. Absolute band errors are averaged across ears and solid-angle weighted over 767 interpolation directions. Contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB error (Contralateral25ERB) uses the same HRIR-domain error but is restricted to great-circle regions of radius $25^\circ$ centered at $(270^\circ,0^\circ)$ for the left ear and $(90^\circ,0^\circ)$ for the right ear, with directional weights renormalized inside each region. We treat these two strict HRIR-domain ERB errors as central metrics of spatial-audio-related band-energy deviation; they differ from the ERB proxy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:training-loss}. ERB-band ILD (ERBBandILDMean) is the average left--right band-energy-difference error over 35 proxy ERB bands centered between 200 and 18000\,Hz and over 72 horizontal-plane interpolation directions.

Interaural time difference follows an implementation compatible with the public FSP-AE pipeline\cite{R4}. Reconstructed and reference HRIRs are low-pass filtered at 1.6\,kHz, resampled to 384\,kHz, and evaluated by the cross-correlation peak within $\pm1$\,ms, without interpolation beyond the peak index. ITD-weighted mean absolute error (ITDWeightedMAE), measured in $\mu$s, solid-angle weights the absolute difference across 767 interpolation directions. The fifth metric is full-sphere log-spectral distortion (FullSphereLSD): the root mean square of the reconstructed-versus-reference log-magnitude difference is computed over frequency, averaged across ears, and solid-angle weighted over 767 interpolation directions.

In addition to the five primary metrics, the CNN ablation uses high-frequency first-order difference error, high-frequency second-order difference error, and multiscale notch-depth error to describe local spectral structure. The first two compare adjacent-bin variations above 4\,kHz, and the notch-depth error uses local depth maps with radii of 4, 8, and 16 bins between 4 and 18\,kHz. All three are solid-angle weighted over 767 interpolation directions. The remaining extended metrics comprise LSD under the LAP24 evaluation pipeline (LAP-LSD)\cite{R19}, maximum absolute ITD error, contralateral high-frequency magnitude error, and horizontal-plane ILD error. LAP-LSD is computed over all 793 directions, including the Q26 observations, without solid-angle weighting. Maximum absolute ITD error uses the same direction-wise estimates as the primary ITD-weighted MAE and takes the largest absolute error over 767 interpolation directions. Contralateral high-frequency error (ContralateralHighFrequency) is the absolute magnitude error in selected bins above 10\,kHz and at or below 20\,kHz within the contralateral hemisphere, with solid-angle weighting and separate normalization for each ear before averaging. Horizontal-plane ILD mean absolute error (HorizontalILDMAE) uses
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{ILD}(\mathbf q)=10\log_{10}
\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{255}|h_L(\mathbf q,n)|^2}
     {\sum_{n=0}^{255}|h_R(\mathbf q,n)|^2},
\end{equation}
and arithmetically averages the absolute predicted-versus-reference ILD difference across the 72 horizontal-plane interpolation directions. This metric uses the broadband energy ratio of complete HRIRs and differs from the primary ERB-band ILD; the two are not interchangeable.

In total, 12 metrics are organized by their role in the paper as five primary metrics, three local-spectral metrics, and four extended metrics. The primary metrics are full-sphere ERB, contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB, ERB-band ILD, ITD-weighted MAE, and full-sphere LSD. The three local-spectral metrics support the CNN ablation, while the four extended metrics further describe broadband spectral and binaural cues under another evaluation domain. Because these metrics describe different error dimensions, we do not construct an aggregate score by adding across units or counting metric wins.

The subject is the independent statistical unit; directions and frequencies do not inflate the sample size. The SONICOM main-method comparison uses 44 test subjects, and the ARI replication uses 22 test subjects. Primary and ARI metrics are reported as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation, whereas local-spectral and extended-metric tables report subject means to control table width. The two strict SONICOM ERB metrics, the CNN ablation, and the ARI experiment additionally use 10000 subject-paired bootstrap resamples. For each of three independent CNN-ablation seeds, the same 44 validation subjects are resampled separately; the repeats are not pooled as 132 independent samples. All subjects are retained, without error-based exclusion.

\subsection{Implementation and Development Settings}

The FiLM-SIREN architecture was selected through a stage-wise training/validation search. For SIREN, we compared frequency mappings, the first-layer frequency coefficient, depth, and width, ultimately choosing joint linear/ERB frequency coordinates, six layers, a hidden width of 256, and $\omega=20$. For FiLM, we compared conditional dimensionality, modulation form, modulation placement, and global/local MCA inputs, and selected a 128-dimensional subject condition with full modulation applied to every sinusoidal layer. This search determines a reproducible configuration but does not claim globally optimal hyperparameters.

The CNN ablation uses 44 validation subjects and compares strictly paired FiLM-SIREN E130 and FSC E190 models across three independent seeds. Each E190 model is obtained by freezing its corresponding E130 model and training the spectral CNN. Epoch counts and hyperparameters are fixed before evaluation and are not reselected using the results. Eight metrics are evaluated, and the paired difference is defined as $\Delta=E_{\mathrm{FSC\ E190}}-E_{\mathrm{FiLM\text{-}SIREN\ E130}}$, so $\Delta<0$ indicates a reduction in error after CNN refinement.

The sparsity experiment uses nested observation sets $Q14\subset Q26\subset Q50$. For Q14 and Q50, MCA-residual training data are rebuilt at the corresponding density and both FiLM-SIREN and the spectral CNN are trained independently; Q26 follows the main experiment's training path. Every model is frozen after training and is not reselected according to test results. To compare densities fairly, all three settings are evaluated on the same 44 test subjects and the same 743 directions, obtained by excluding the 50 Q50 observations from the 793-direction grid. The Q26 primary result uses its own 767 unobserved directions, whereas this experiment uses the common 743-direction domain; the two Q26 values therefore need not be identical.

The ARI external replication keeps the FSC architecture, losses, optimizer, and E130/E190 epoch budgets unchanged but inherits neither SONICOM parameters nor normalization statistics. FiLM-SIREN and the spectral CNN are trained from scratch using ARI training subjects. The experiment uses one prespecified seed, and the authoritative model is the endpoint after E190. The validation set monitors training integrity only and does not alter the budget or select a checkpoint. Model identity, the five primary metrics, and the statistical criterion were frozen before test access, and the protocol was neither retrained nor adjusted in response to test results. This design evaluates replication after retraining on a new database rather than zero-shot transfer of a frozen SONICOM model.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first analyze FSC residual compensation relative to MCA, then compare learning-based methods, and finally examine local-spectral and extended metrics, the CNN ablation, the sparsity experiment, and external replication on ARI. The SONICOM main-method comparison and sparsity experiment use 44 test subjects, the CNN ablation uses only 44 validation subjects, and the ARI experiment uses 22 independent test subjects. The main text emphasizes subject means and sample standard deviations and reports 95\% bootstrap intervals where paired statistics were specified.

\subsection{Comparison with MCA}

FSC shows clear magnitude and spectral improvements over MCA on the five primary metrics, but the temporal cue does not improve in parallel. In Table~\ref{tab:primary-results}, full-sphere ERB decreases from 1.0822 to 0.8360\,dB, contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB from 1.7458 to 1.2280\,dB, ERB-band ILD from 2.0562 to 1.6514\,dB, and full-sphere LSD from 4.7914 to 3.6029\,dB. Subject-paired results for the two strict ERB metrics agree with the mean trend: the mean FSC--MCA difference is $-0.2462$\,dB with a 95\% interval of $[-0.2942,-0.1665]$ for full-sphere ERB, and $-0.5178$\,dB with an interval of $[-0.5728,-0.4541]$ for contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB. Both metrics improve for 43 of 44 subjects. In contrast, ITD-weighted MAE increases from 18.3997 to 18.8480\,$\mu$s, so FSC is not interpreted as an ITD-improvement method.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Trade-off Between Spectral Refinement and Binaural Cues}

Residual learning after MCA provides clear magnitude and spectral compensation in this study. Table~\ref{tab:primary-results} shows that FSC has lower mean full-sphere ERB, contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB, ERB-band ILD, and full-sphere LSD than MCA, while its ITD-weighted MAE is slightly higher. Relative to learning-based methods, FSC has lower means on the first three magnitude or level metrics, but FSP-AE has lower ITD and full-sphere LSD, and RANF also has lower full-sphere LSD. FSC is therefore better at correcting MCA residuals in auditory-band and binaural-level errors, while broadband LSD and temporal cues do not become optimal automatically with the ERB objective. We neither judge overall superiority by counting metric wins nor interpret objective error reductions directly as improved localization or externalization.

Local spectral shape, binaural energy relationships, and ITD reflect different reconstruction requirements. First- and second-order differences and multiscale notch depth constrain local spectral variation, whereas absolute magnitude and ERB errors assess deviations at other scales. FSC's low means on the three local endpoints can coexist with FSP-AE's advantage in contralateral high-frequency and maximum ITD error. The local endpoints resemble auxiliary training terms, so their improvements show that the model performs well on explicitly constrained objectives but do not establish a general auditory benefit. Low full-sphere ERB and ERB-band ILD in the external main comparison likewise do not guarantee stable strict horizontal-plane ILD or ITD gains. The former is based on the binaural energy ratio of complete reconstructed HRIRs, while the latter is estimated by low-pass cross-correlation. FSC does not predict ITD directly, and jointly inputting binaural information and adding an ILD loss do not guarantee lower errors for every subject.

\subsection{Structural Roles and Model Complexity}

The FiLM, SIREN, and CNN modules respectively provide subject-level conditioning, a continuous residual representation, and cross-frequency binaural refinement, jointly producing the final residual along one forward path. Unlike subject-specific model fitting, the conditional encoder compresses each subject's sparse binaural spatial observations into an embedding of shared dimensionality, enabling a single cross-subject model to express individual differences through FiLM modulation. This is the key interface connecting subject acoustic observations to the continuous neural field. In the validation-set CNN ablation, FSC E190 yields negative paired differences relative to FiLM-SIREN E130 on all eight metrics across three independent seeds, with all 24 upper confidence limits below zero; the three local-spectral metrics improve consistently in all three repeats (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn-ablation}). The result supports a stable incremental benefit from the CNN-refinement stage but does not isolate the causal contribution of each auxiliary loss.

Zero initialization of the CNN output layer only guarantees that the function of the complete FSC equals the stage-one FiLM-SIREN configuration at the start of stage two; it does not guarantee monotonically decreasing errors during training. FSC inference always includes all three modules. The Q26 model contains 1.519 million deployed parameters and occupies approximately 6.45\,MiB on disk. Inference efficiency was measured on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 in FP32 for 793 directions $\times$ 463 frequency bins. After five warm-up runs, 30 timed runs yielded a median model-inference latency of 110.45\,ms and a P95 latency of 110.95\,ms. These timings exclude checkpoint loading and disk I/O and are reported only to characterize the computational cost of FSC; because the external baselines were not benchmarked under the same implementation and hardware conditions, no cross-method efficiency advantage is claimed.

\subsection{Generalization Scope and Limitations}

In the SONICOM sparsity experiment with 44 test subjects and 743 shared directions, every reported mean error decreases as the observation density increases through Q14, Q26, and Q50. Full-sphere ERB decreases from 0.9535 to 0.8318 and 0.7931\,dB, while the main ITD reduction occurs between Q14 and Q26, suggesting that additional measurements have different marginal effects across evaluation dimensions. This trend remains specific to the fixed test cohort and current spatial sampling schemes.

The ARI experiment examines database transferability from another perspective. Prior work shows that HRTF databases may retain identifiable measurement-system differences even after basic harmonization, and that dedicated cross-database frequency-response normalization can further reduce them\cite{R24,R25}. When all parameters are reestimated from ARI training data while retaining the architecture, training objective, and E130/E190 epoch budgets, every paired FSC--MCA interval is below zero and all five metrics improve for all 22 subjects. This supports reproducibility of residual compensation on a second database, especially for magnitude and spectral effects in ERB, ERB-band ILD, and LSD. However, independent training, normalization, and ARI-Q26 mean that the experiment does not show direct transfer of a frozen SONICOM model without adaptation. Strict zero-shot cross-database generalization remains to be evaluated separately.

The composite objective uses an ERB proxy as its central spectral term together with high-frequency error, ILD, spectral differences, and notch-depth constraints. Its main contribution is an auditory-band-oriented objective design for sparse HRTF residual refinement, rather than a claim that one fixed loss formulation is definitive. Future work can investigate differentiable objectives that more closely match strict HRIR-domain ERB, adaptive multi-objective balancing, and perceptual losses.

Several limitations remain. The ARI experiment evaluates replication after retraining on the target database and does not answer the zero-shot cross-database question. FSC reuses the MCA phase and does not directly predict ITD, so temporal-cue capability is constrained by the front-end reconstruction. The Q26 model has approximately 1.519 million deployed parameters, and a uniform implementation and hardware comparison against every baseline has not been completed. The CNN ablation evaluates the incremental effect of the complete refinement stage but does not provide term-by-term causal attribution of the auxiliary losses. The ARI replication uses only one independent training seed, and its subject bootstrap does not quantify training randomness. Finally, no subjective listening experiment is currently available, so objective ERB, ILD, ITD, or LSD improvements cannot be equated directly with localization accuracy, externalization, or preference.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We proposed FiLM-conditioned SIREN with Spectral CNN (FSC) to compensate for direction-dependent magnitude residuals remaining after MCA interpolation. Sparse binaural spatial observations from each subject are compressed into a fixed-dimensional embedding, which modulates a continuous SIREN residual field through FiLM, and a binaural spectral CNN further corrects cross-frequency structure. The MCA phase is then reused to reconstruct the HRIR. In parallel, strict HRIR-domain ERB error is adopted as a central evaluation dimension, and an ERB proxy is introduced during training so that learning emphasizes magnitude errors meaningful on an auditory-band scale.

Under the SONICOM Q26 test protocol, FSC obtains full-sphere ERB, contralateral-$25^\circ$ ERB, ERB-band ILD, ITD-weighted MAE, and full-sphere LSD values of 0.8360, 1.2280, 1.6514\,dB, 18.8480\,$\mu$s, and 3.6029\,dB. Relative to MCA, FSC has lower means on three magnitude or level metrics and full-sphere LSD but slightly higher ITD-weighted MAE. Relative to FSP-AE and RANF, FSC is lower on the first three metrics, while FSP-AE is lower on ITD and full-sphere LSD and RANF is also lower on full-sphere LSD. In the CNN ablation, all eight paired differences are negative with 95\% intervals below zero across three independent seeds. In the sparsity experiment on 743 shared directions, every reported mean error decreases through Q14, Q26, and Q50, with full-sphere ERB values of 0.9535, 0.8318, and 0.7931\,dB. In the external ARI replication, FSC improves all five primary metrics for all 22 test subjects, and all five 95\% paired-bootstrap intervals lie below zero, showing that retraining the method on a second database can stably reduce MCA residuals.

The current method remains limited by the absence of zero-shot cross-database validation, dependence on the MCA phase, lack of a uniform inference-cost comparison against all baselines, absence of term-wise loss ablations, a single training seed in the ARI replication, and the lack of a listening experiment. Future work will focus on more consistent ERB and perceptual objectives, zero-shot cross-database generalization, loss-mechanism analysis, and subjective spatial-hearing validation.
